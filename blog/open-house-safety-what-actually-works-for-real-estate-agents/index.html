Open House Safety: What Actually Works, and What Just Feels Safe

By Connor MacIvor · 23 years LAPD, 27 years in California real estate · July 23, 2026 · 11 minute read

Key Takeaways

  • The risk is structural, not dramatic. Any one open house is probably fine. A career of being alone in empty buildings with strangers is a different calculation.
  • The highest value habit costs one text message. Tell a specific person the address and the time you expect to be done.
  • Positioning beats reaction. Where you park, where you stand, and which way you walk decide more than anything you could do once a situation has started.
  • Verification protects the seller too, which is exactly why it survives as a habit when safety-only rules get skipped.
  • A policy nobody can follow is not a policy. If it does not live in the tools you already open, it will lose to a busy Sunday.

I spent 23 years with the Los Angeles Police Department and 27 years selling California real estate, which means I have stood on both sides of this problem. I have been the person who shows up after something went wrong, and I have been the agent alone in a vacant house on a Sunday afternoon wondering about the guy who just walked in without signing anything.

The safety conversation in this industry tends to swing between two useless poles. One is denial, the shrug that says nothing has ever happened to me. The other is fear, the pitch that turns every stranger into a threat and sells you something. Neither helps you on Sunday.

What follows is the middle. Which precautions genuinely change outcomes, which ones only reduce anxiety, and how to build a routine you will still be doing in six months.

How Dangerous Is an Open House Really?

Any single open house is very probably fine, and framing it that way is the mistake. The correct question is not what happens today. It is what the exposure looks like across a career.

The job description, stated plainly, is this: go alone to an empty building, unlock it, publicly advertise that you will be inside it at a known time, and admit anyone who arrives, including people who found you through an internet listing and whose names you have never verified. Do this most weekends for twenty years.

Written down like that, every other industry would call it a controlled-access problem and write a protocol. Real estate mostly calls it Sunday.

Roughly 31 percent of agents report feeling unsafe at open houses, and about 75,000 Realtors were victims of a crime in 2023. I am not putting those numbers here to frighten anyone. I am putting them here because agents routinely treat their own discomfort as a character flaw, something to push past and not mention. It is not. It is an accurate read of a real structural exposure, and the correct response to an accurate read is a system, not more courage.

Low probability multiplied by high frequency, across a career, is exactly the risk profile that protocols exist for. You do not wear a seatbelt because you expect a crash today.

The practices that work share one trait: they operate before anything happens. The ones that mostly provide comfort operate after, when your options have already narrowed.

Why the paper sign in sheet does not count

A clipboard by the door verifies nothing. Anybody can write anything, most handwriting is unreadable a week later, and everyone in the room knows it is not checked. It creates the feeling of a record without creating a record.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A visible, verified check in changes behavior at the door, because a person with bad intent is deciding in that moment whether this is an easy place to be anonymous. A clipboard answers yes. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why the format of the check in matters more than the fact of it.

What Is the Single Most Effective Safety Habit?

Tell one specific person the property address and the exact time you expect to be finished. That is it. It costs one text message and it outperforms almost everything else you could do.

Here is why it is disproportionately powerful. Nearly all safety advice is about surviving a confrontation. This one is about shortening how long you are alone in it, and that variable moves outcomes more than anything you could do with your hands.

Two details decide whether it works.

Name a person, not a group. A message to a team chat is everyone's responsibility, which means it is nobody's. Pick one human being who will notice.

Name a time, not a vibe. "I will be at 123 Elm until about 4" is a plan. "Heading out to an open house" is a status update. The time is the trigger. Without it there is nothing for anyone to notice.

If you do nothing else from this article, do that. Everything below improves your odds. That one habit is the difference between someone realizing in twenty minutes and someone realizing at dinner.

How Do You Set Up a Property Before Anyone Arrives?

You set up the room while the house is empty and your judgment is clear, because you will not be thinking about exits once you are mid-conversation with a stranger about the kitchen island.

Park at the curb, facing out

Never in the driveway. You want to be able to leave without a three point turn and without being blocked in by whoever parks behind you. This costs nothing, works every time, and takes about four seconds of thought.

Walk the property first

Before the first visitor, walk every room and note the second way out. Unlock the exits you might need. Find where the cell signal is weak, because that is where you do not want to end up standing.

Decide where you will stand

Pick positions near doorways with the room in front of you. This is not paranoia, it is the same instinct that makes you sit facing the door in a restaurant. It just needs to be a decision instead of an accident.

Walk behind, not in front

Let visitors move ahead of you through the house. The tour is better anyway, because they explore instead of following. More importantly, you never end up in a back bedroom with a person between you and the only door.

New agents get taught to lead the tour. It feels like good service. It puts you in the worst possible position in every room you enter, and it is the single easiest habit on this page to reverse.

Why Does Verification Protect the Seller Too?

A real check in creates a record of who entered the property, and that record serves the seller as directly as it serves the agent. This dual purpose is the reason verification survives as a habit when purely safety-focused rules quietly get dropped.

Think about what the seller actually wants after an open house. Not a photo of a clipboard. They want to know how many people came through, who they were, and whether anyone was serious. A verified check in answers all three and turns a safety measure into a reporting feature you can hand over as part of your service.

That is the honest reason to build safety into the workflow rather than bolt it on. A precaution that only protects you competes with your job. A precaution that also improves your client's experience becomes part of your job. Only one of those survives a busy season.

What Is a Duress Code and Why Does It Work?

A duress code is an ordinary sounding word or phrase, agreed in advance with whoever is monitoring you, that means send help now. Spoken aloud it sounds like nothing. To your contact it is unambiguous.

It works because of a constraint people rarely think through until they are inside it. If someone is standing next to you, you cannot make a call that sounds like a call for help. Anything that visibly signals alarm escalates the situation you are trying to defuse. A duress code is the only signal that is safe to send while the person is still in the room.

Two rules make it real. Agree on it in advance with a specific person, and never use it casually. A code that has been used as a joke is a code your contact will hesitate on, and hesitation is the entire thing you were trying to eliminate.

Why Do Brokerage Safety Policies Get Ignored?

Because most of them live in an annual training video instead of in the tools agents open every day.

I have watched this pattern for decades. A brokerage responds to an incident with a policy. The policy is genuinely well written. It is presented once, acknowledged in a signature, filed, and then it competes against a Sunday with three showings, a lender call, and an inspection deadline. It loses. It always loses, and the reason is not that agents do not care about their own safety.

The reason is that the policy asked them to remember a separate process. Anything that requires remembering a separate process under time pressure will be skipped, in every industry, by every population of professionals. That is not a discipline problem. It is a design problem.

Safety practices survive when they are attached to something you already have to do. Checking a visitor in. Sending the seller a report. That is not a convenience feature. That is the adoption mechanism.

This is the whole reason ShoSafe exists and it is worth being plain about it. The problem was never that agents lack safety information. It is everywhere, most of it is decent, and none of it is in the workflow. Agent safety should be boring infrastructure that runs in the background, not an annual video everyone clicks through.

A Routine You Will Actually Follow

Five steps. If it takes longer than this you will stop doing it by March.

  1. Text one person the address and your expected finish time.
  2. Park at the curb, facing out.
  3. Walk the property before anyone arrives. Note exits, unlock what you need, find the dead spots.
  4. Check people in at the door so there is a real record.
  5. Walk behind, never in front. Keep the exit on your side.

None of that requires equipment, a subscription, or courage. It requires deciding once and then doing it the same way every time, which is what a protocol is.
